# Global Ride — Adversarial Business Review

This document records the questions the project must survive before its commercial thesis should be treated as validated.

## 1. The incumbent advantage is liquidity

A lower platform fee does not automatically create a better marketplace. Passengers care about availability, wait time, price, reliability and trust. Drivers care about ride volume as well as commission. An incumbent can remain economically attractive even with a higher fee if it generates materially more paid utilization.

**Test:** compare driver net economics per online hour, not only fee percentage.

## 2. A subscription can create the wrong incentive

Predictable fees are attractive to highly active drivers but may be unattractive to occasional drivers. A model optimized only for high-volume supply can make early driver acquisition harder.

**Test:** segment economics by low-, medium- and high-activity drivers before locking the pricing structure.

## 3. Cheap software does not mean cheap operations

Maps, cloud infrastructure and payment APIs may be manageable at prototype scale. Marketplace operations can be much more expensive: acquisition, incentives, customer support, fraud, refunds, compliance, insurance-related processes and local operations.

**Test:** include operational cost and acquisition cost in contribution economics; never compare only subscription revenue with hosting cost.

## 4. Passenger acquisition can erase the fee advantage

A driver-side economic advantage is not useful if the platform has to subsidize passenger demand heavily enough to destroy contribution margin.

**Test:** track acquisition cost, repeat rate and rides per acquired passenger during the pilot.

## 5. Multi-homing is both an opportunity and a threat

Drivers can use several platforms. That lowers the barrier to trying Global Ride, but it also means they can immediately prioritize whichever platform supplies the next profitable ride.

**Test:** measure acceptance, online availability and completed rides rather than registrations.

## 6. Regulation is part of the product

A ride-hailing launch is not simply a software launch. Local transport, taxi, pricing, licensing, tax, data-protection and payment rules can materially change the business model.

**Test:** complete jurisdiction-specific legal and tax review before any live commercial pilot.

## 7. The model needs a wedge

Launching everywhere at once maximizes the cold-start problem. A defensible pilot should concentrate supply and demand where the product can become reliably useful.

Possible wedges to validate include a constrained geography, airport-oriented demand, specific operator partnerships or another repeatable high-frequency use case. No wedge should be declared the winner before evidence exists.

## Kill criteria

The team should be willing to change or abandon the initial pricing thesis if evidence shows that:

- driver savings do not improve supply retention or availability;
- passenger wait times remain uncompetitive at sustainable acquisition spend;
- platform revenue cannot cover realistic operating and acquisition costs;
- regulation makes the proposed operating structure impractical;
- drivers value ride volume enough that the fee advantage does not change behavior;
- the marketplace requires permanent subsidies to maintain liquidity.

A credible business thesis is one that can be disproved. These tests are designed to make Global Ride stronger, not to protect the original idea from evidence.
