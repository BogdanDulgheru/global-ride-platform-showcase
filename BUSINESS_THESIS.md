# Global Ride — Public Business Thesis

## The question

Can ride-hailing be structured so active drivers and operators have a more predictable relationship with platform costs while the platform still builds a sustainable business?

## Why build before pitching

Global Ride's first milestone was to make the marketplace workflow tangible. A working prototype now demonstrates the passenger and driver journey. This reduces one category of risk — whether the core product interaction can be implemented — while leaving the harder commercial questions to validation.

## The hypothesis

A different platform-cost structure may improve economic alignment for highly active supply. That hypothesis only matters if Global Ride can also solve the marketplace problems that determine whether ride-hailing works: passenger demand, driver availability, trust, safety, reliability, support, payments and local operations.

## What would falsify the thesis

The model should be rejected or changed if driver savings do not materially improve retention/supply, if platform revenue cannot support acquisition and operations, if passengers do not receive competitive availability and pricing, or if the cost of creating marketplace liquidity overwhelms the economic advantage.

## Validation roadmap

1. Validate driver/operator willingness to switch or multi-home.
2. Model contribution economics under low, medium and high driver activity.
3. Test a geographically narrow pilot rather than a broad launch.
4. Measure supply availability, request acceptance, completed rides and repeat passenger behavior.
5. Compare acquisition and support costs with platform revenue.
6. Refine pricing only after observed behavior replaces assumptions.

## Disclosure boundary

This public document communicates the thesis, not the recipe. Detailed pricing tiers, thresholds, cost assumptions, unit economics, rollout mechanics and source implementation remain private for partner/investor discussions.
## Public benchmark: Austria

Bolt currently publishes an 18% commission on the final order price in Austria. That gives the thesis a concrete benchmark.

| Monthly gross ride value | 18% benchmark platform fee |
| ---: | ---: |
| €2,000 | €360 |
| €3,000 | €540 |
| €4,000 | €720 |
| €5,000 | €900 |
| €6,000 | €1,080 |
| €8,000 | €1,440 |

These figures are a simple benchmark calculation, not an estimate of typical driver turnover and not a claim about Global Ride pricing.

The strategic question is whether Global Ride can capture enough recurring platform revenue to fund a reliable marketplace while allowing highly active supply to retain meaningfully more of its ride value.

## The hard part: marketplace economics

Software is not the primary risk. A technically strong ride-hailing product can still fail if it cannot create local liquidity. The pilot therefore has to prove both sides at the same time: enough available drivers for acceptable passenger wait times and enough passenger demand to keep participating drivers economically interested.

This is why the launch thesis favors a narrow geographic wedge over a broad citywide promise. Density first; expansion second.

## Private decision model

The internal model should evaluate at least these variables before pricing is locked:

- active drivers and active operators
- monthly gross ride value per active driver
- platform revenue per active driver
- payment-processing burden and who bears it
- maps/routing and infrastructure cost per completed ride
- support, fraud, insurance/compliance and operational overhead
- passenger and driver acquisition cost
- incentives required to create initial liquidity
- churn and driver multi-homing
- completed rides, acceptance rate and utilization

The public repository intentionally does not disclose the proposed subscription thresholds, progressive charging formula or target contribution margins.

## Scale gates

**100 active drivers — pilot gate.** Prove that supply can be recruited, passengers can reliably obtain rides in a narrow operating zone, and the fee proposition is understood.

**1,000 active drivers — city economics gate.** Prove repeat passenger demand, retention, support processes and positive contribution economics without relying indefinitely on subsidies.

**10,000 active drivers — expansion gate.** Only after the marketplace model is repeatable should infrastructure, operations and geographic expansion be designed for larger scale.

The number of registered accounts is not the success metric. Active supply, completed rides, repeat demand and contribution economics are.
