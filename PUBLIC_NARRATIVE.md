# Global Ride — Public Narrative Guide

This guide defines how Global Ride should be presented publicly to recruiters, potential investors, partners and technical reviewers.

## One-line story

**Global Ride is a working ride-hailing product prototype exploring whether a different platform relationship can create stronger economic alignment between the marketplace and active supply.**

## The narrative

### 1. Start with the question

Ride-hailing is a marketplace problem, not only a mobile-app problem. Global Ride asks whether the economic relationship between the platform and participating supply can be structured differently while preserving the convenience passengers expect.

### 2. Show that the idea became software

The product already demonstrates a connected passenger and driver journey: destination search, route calculation, ride categories, request and acceptance, pickup, active trip, completion, rating, tip, demo payment and receipt.

### 3. Be precise about maturity

Global Ride is a working prototype in pre-validation. It is not presented as a live transportation network, a production-scale system or a business with proven traction.

### 4. Explain the opportunity without attacking anyone

The project studies marketplace economics and alternative platform-cost structures. It does not need to criticize or identify another company to explain why this question is commercially interesting.

### 5. End with two doors

**Engineering:** evaluate the architecture, product thinking and execution; controlled private-source review can be discussed when appropriate.

**Investment / partnership:** discuss the private unit-economics model, pilot design, commercialization assumptions and validation plan.

## Language rules

### Use

- working prototype
- pre-validation
- product thesis
- marketplace economics
- established platforms
- percentage-based fee model
- illustrative scenario
- hypothesis to validate
- controlled technical review

### Avoid

- "better than" a named competitor
- "cheaper than" a named competitor
- claims that another company overcharges, exploits or harms participants
- unsupported market-share, driver-income or competitor-profit claims
- "disrupt", "revolutionary", "guaranteed", "industry-leading" unless evidence later supports a narrowly defined claim
- production-ready, scalable or secure as absolute claims before those properties are demonstrated

## Disclosure boundary

### Public

Product story, screenshots, working flows, high-level architecture, technology categories, demonstrated prototype capabilities, validation roadmap and generic illustrative economics.

### Private

Complete source code, credentials, internal endpoints, detailed pricing thresholds, progressive charging formula, unit-economics assumptions, contribution targets, launch mechanics, partner terms and commercially sensitive implementation details.

## Evidence rule

Every public factual claim should be traceable to one of three things: a demonstrated product capability, an explicitly labelled illustrative calculation, or a reliable public source. Anything else should be written as a hypothesis, objective or planned validation step.
